# Book Seat Shapes

The thirteen starting shapes, and where they come from.

These are the seat archetypes the shipped `panel.json` declares. They are a
STARTING ROSTER, not a fixed cast: an agency's own `book-profile.md` decides
which survive, which merge, and which are replaced. Every one of them ships
`active: false` and becomes active only when a real dossier is written from the
agency's own evidence.

## Where these shapes come from — and what that is worth

The shapes are drawn from the client mix that independent Long Island agencies
verifiably carry: predominantly small agencies (US Census County Business
Patterns 2023 counts 1,659 insurance-agency establishments across Nassau and
Suffolk, 453 of them in the 5-19 employee band), writing a mixed personal and
commercial book, with commercial weighted to contractors, restaurants, and small
landlords.

**What that evidence supports:** that agencies of this size and mix exist in
quantity, and that the commercial shapes below are common rather than invented.

**What it does not support:** anything about how any actual policyholder reacts
to a letter. The density research counted firms; it did not interview clients.
Every behavioral claim in a shape below is a MODELED ASSUMPTION until the
agency's own evidence or a calibrated mailing supports it, and every dossier
written from these shapes must label it that way.

That distinction is the difference between a research instrument and a
convincing-sounding fiction. Do not let it blur.

## The core seats

**1. Tenured personal lines loyalist** (`buyer`, `core`)
Twenty or thirty years with the agency, auto and homeowners, has never once
shopped. Opens agency mail because it has always been routine. The risk in the
letter is not price — it is the first moment they wonder whether the agency has
stopped paying attention to them. Once that thought lands, a mailer from a
competitor six weeks later gets read instead of binned.

**2. Contractor with a general liability jump** (`buyer`, `core`)
Feels every commercial renewal directly in a job's margin, and is often bidding
work whose price already assumed last year's number. Wants to know what changed
and whether it was shopped, in that order, and will call the producer directly
rather than the office. Reads slowly and remembers exact figures.

**3. Restaurant owner who reads the premium line only** (`buyer`, `core`)
Opens the letter between deliveries, finds the number, and forms the entire
opinion from it. Everything above the number was not read. If the reason lives
in paragraph three, it does not exist. Tests whether the letter survives being
skimmed by someone with ninety seconds.

**4. Multi-entity landlord** (`buyer`, `core`)
Three or four LLCs, several buildings, and one email address for all of it.
First question is always which entity this is about, and a letter that does not
say plainly gets set aside to deal with later, which means never. Tests whether
the letter identifies itself.

**5. Price shopper already holding a quote** (`buyer`, `core`)
Has a competitor's number in hand before the agency's letter arrives. The letter
is not being read on its own; it is being read against that number. The only
question is whether anything here is worth a phone call first.

**6. Small commercial owner-operator** (`buyer`, `core`)
Runs the business and handles the insurance in the evening. Not price-blind but
time-poor. Defers anything that looks like it needs thought, and deferral past
the renewal date is functionally a decision.

**7. Recent claim client** (`buyer`, `core`)
Filed a claim in the past year and is now reading an increase letter. Will
assume, unless the letter is explicit, that this is retaliation for the claim.
This seat is the reason a stated reason matters: it is the single most common
avoidable non-renewal in the shapes here, and the most common defect is a letter
that never separates the two.

**8. Office manager who handles the mail** (`user`, `core`)
On commercial accounts the letter usually reaches the owner through someone
else, who decides how urgent it looks. A letter that reads as routine gets filed
and surfaces after the renewal date. This seat is not the buyer and is
frequently the deciding factor anyway.

## The adjacent seats

**9. Cross-sell target, auto only** (`buyer`, `adjacent`)
Has auto with the agency and homeowners somewhere else, usually for a forgotten
reason. Not hostile, just unbothered. Tests whether a cross-sell letter gives a
reason that is about their situation rather than the agency's.

**10. Referring accountant** (`influencer`, `adjacent`)
Sends clients to this agency and sees this letter over one of their shoulders.
Does not buy anything; decides whether the next referral happens. A letter that
embarrasses a referral source costs more than the mailing earns.

## The specialist

**11. Compliance and claims reviewer** (`specialist`, `adjacent`)
Not a customer and never counted as one. Reads only for statements the agency
cannot support in writing: implied coverage the policy does not provide,
characterizations of a carrier's motives, comparisons the agency cannot back,
guarantees about future pricing or claim outcomes, and anything the carrier
agreement or state filing constrains.

Returns `PASS` or `FLAG` with `DECISION: NOT-APPLICABLE`. A `FLAG` caps the
result no matter what the total says. A `PASS` means only that this modeled gate
found no issue in this letter — it is not a compliance review and does not
replace the agency's licensed judgment or its carrier relationship.

## If the agency writes personal lines only

Count the shapes above before assuming the roster fits. Eight of the thirteen are
commercial or cross-sell shapes, which leaves a personal-lines-only agency five
seats — the bottom of the 5-15 range with no room to cut, and no `wrong-fit`
seat at all if the agency never cross-sells. That agency is not an edge case;
plenty of Long Island books are personal lines and nothing else.

**Do not pad the commercial seats to reach a count, and do not run a four-seat
panel.** Replace them. These shapes are drawn from the same book profile
questions and stand in one-for-one:

**A. The household losing coverage entirely** (`buyer`, `core`)
The carrier is non-renewing the area, not the person, and there may be no
replacement market that takes everyone. Reads the letter for one thing: am I
going to be uninsured. Price is a distant second and a letter that leads with
price has misread the room. This is the shape a coastal or wildfire-exposed book
carries in quantity, and it is the letter agencies most often get wrong, because
a notice about LOSING something is not a notice about paying more.

**B. The retiree on a fixed income** (`buyer`, `core`)
Any increase is measured against a monthly figure that does not move. Has been
with the agency a long time, will not shop out of loyalty, and will quietly go
without something else instead — which the agency never learns. Calls the office
rather than emailing, and expects a person.

**C. The multi-generational family account** (`buyer`, `core`)
The agency insures the parents and both grown children. One letter to one
household is read by three, and a letter that lands badly costs the agency three
accounts, not one. Tests whether the letter survives being forwarded.

**D. The inherited-policy holder** (`buyer`, `adjacent`)
Kept the agency because a parent used it, has no relationship of their own, and
is the likeliest in the book to shop on any excuse. The weakest tie the agency
has, and usually invisible on the aging report.

**E. The first-time homeowner** (`user`, `adjacent`)
Bought recently, does not know what a hurricane deductible is, and will not admit
it. Reads the letter for what to DO. Every unexplained term is a place they stop
reading. Tests plain language harder than any other seat.

The dissent seats below are still both required. Where the agency does not
cross-sell, read the `wrong-fit` seat as its general form — the client the
letter's ask genuinely does not suit — and see the note under seat 13.

## The seats who must be allowed to say no

Both stay active. An agency asking to cut them is asking for a formatter, and
the panel loses the thing it exists for.

**12. Already leaving regardless** (`buyer`, `hard-no`)
Decided before the envelope arrived — bought a house, sold the business, a
relative got licensed, an old grievance that was never raised. Says no to a
well-written letter, and MUST remain able to. If a rewrite converts this seat,
something is wrong with the panel, not right with the letter.

**13. Wrong cross-sell target** (`buyer`, `wrong-fit`)
The person the offer genuinely does not suit: pitched a commercial line for a
business they closed, a landlord product for the house they live in, a life
product against a situation nobody asked about. Should decline.

**A yes from this seat is a failure, not a win.** It means the letter is
persuasive enough to produce a sale that should not happen — a policy that
fits badly, a client who feels sold to on the renewal after next, and a
retention problem the agency created for itself. Report it that way in the
synthesis, explicitly, because the instinct on reading a positive reaction is to
count it.

**When the agency does not cross-sell at all**, this seat still exists, because
the rule is not about cross-selling — it is that SOMETHING the letter asks for
must be wrong for somebody, or nothing in the panel can catch a letter that
persuades the wrong person. Take the general form: **the client the letter's ask
genuinely does not suit.** On a renewal letter urging everyone to call and
reshop, it is the household whose coverage is already correct and who would be
talked into changing something that was fine. On a non-renewal notice, it is the
client for whom the replacement being offered is genuinely worse than going to
the residual market. On a win-back note, it is the client who left for a good
reason that still holds.

The test for whether a wrong-fit seat is real: **its yes must be bad news.** A
seat whose yes the agency would be pleased about is a core seat wearing the
label, and a panel with one of those has quietly lost its dissent coverage while
still passing validation.
