# Insurance Book-Roll Panel — Operations Manual

Written for the person who actually writes the letters. No technical background
assumed, and nothing here requires you to open a terminal.

## What this is

You are about to mail one letter to a few hundred clients. Once it goes, it is
gone. This reads that letter back to you through thirteen of your own clients
first — not real ones, but carefully built stand-ins for the kinds of people who
are actually in your book.

Each of the thirteen reads your letter on their own, without knowing what you
are hoping for, and tells you whether they would pick up the phone and why. Then
a separate reviewer shows you where they agreed, where they did not, and who was
about to start shopping you.

The moment this earns its money is usually the same: you find out that nine of
thirteen read your renewal letter as a price-increase notice, while there is
still time to fix it.

## What it does not do

It does not give insurance advice, read your policy language, price anything,
or tell you whether a carrier move is a good idea. It does not send anything.
You are the author, you are the licensed professional, and your name is the one
on the letter.

## Setting up, once

Say **"build a book panel."** You will be asked for two things:

1. **Your book.** What lines you write, roughly how it splits between personal
   and commercial, and the four or five kinds of client you deal with most. Ten
   minutes of plain answers.
2. **What you have noticed.** Which past letter lit up the phones, what people
   said when they called, what clients said on the way out. This is the most
   valuable thing you can hand it, and it is the part most agencies skip.

Rough answers are fine. Made-up ones are not, and it will not make them up for
you — see "When it says no" below.

Then it builds thirteen client stand-ins from your answers and shows you the
list before writing anything long. Look at it. If a shape is missing from your
book, say so; if one does not exist in your book, cut it.

**Two of the thirteen are there to say no.** One client is leaving regardless of
what you write, and one is being offered something that genuinely does not suit
them. Leave them in. A panel where everybody likes your letter tells you
nothing, and those two are how you know the rest of it is honest.

## Reading a letter

Send the letter and say **"wash this letter."** A rough draft is fine.

Real names, policy numbers, and premium figures get swapped for placeholders
first, and you will be told it happened. The panel reads how the letter is
written; it never needs a real person's details.

Then the thirteen read it separately and you get back:

- what each one decided, and their reaction in their own words;
- where they agreed and, more usefully, where they did not;
- which of them read it as bad news;
- anything the compliance reviewer thinks you cannot support in writing;
- a score, and a plain recommendation: mail it, fix it, or stop.

## When it says no

**This kit will refuse to run, and that is the point.** It stops in three
situations:

1. **You have not given it your own letter.** It will not practise on the
   examples that came with it. Those are there so you can see how this works
   before you risk a real mailing; a run against them would tell you nothing
   about your clients.
2. **The book profile is empty.** Without knowing what your book carries, the
   thirteen stand-ins are thirteen generic strangers and their reactions are
   about insurance in general, not about you.
3. **Your letter says the premium is changing but never says how much or why.**
   This is the most common problem in a renewal letter and the one that
   generates the calls you did not want.

On that third one you have two ways forward, and both are fine: give it the
number and the reason, or tell it you are leaving them out on purpose. If you
choose the omission, the AI records that explicit choice in the gate, runs the
letter exactly as written, and shows you what the omission costs. What it will
not do is invent a figure or a cause to get past the gap.

When it stops, it tells you what it could read, what it could not, and the one
question that unblocks it. That is the kit doing its job.

## Fixing and re-reading

Say **"rewrite and retest."** You get three genuinely different versions — the
smallest fix to what you wrote, one that leads with the reason instead of the
number, and one with a different structure or a different ask — each read by the
same thirteen so the comparison means something.

It stops after three rounds. If nothing has improved by then, the problem is the
number, the timing, or the offer, not the words, and it will say so instead of
polishing forever.

## After you mail it

This is the ten minutes that makes everything above worth trusting. Six weeks
after the drop, say **"calibrate the panel"** and tell it what actually
happened — how many called, what they said, who did not renew.

That gets written down beside what the panel predicted. Do that three or four
times and you stop guessing about whether this thing is any good, because you
will have the record.

## How to read the results

- A client saying no is often the most useful thing in the report.
- **The wrong-fit client saying yes is a problem, not a win.** It means the
  letter is persuasive enough to sell something that does not fit, and you pay
  for that at the renewal after next.
- Disagreement between the thirteen is more useful than agreement.
- All thirteen positive is a warning that something is wrong with the panel.
- The score is directional. The mailing is what proves it.

## What it is honestly worth

Thirteen careful stand-ins reading your letter is a great deal better than
mailing it unread, and it is not a prediction of what your book will do. It
does not replace calling five clients and asking them, and if you have time for
that, do that too. What it does is catch the letter that reads as bad news
before four hundred people get it.
